Homepage

  • In Unit C and Unit D we saw that binomial and Poisson regression may suffer from overdispersion: the data are more variable than the model allows.

  • This unit presents quasi-likelihoods (Wedderburn 1974), a simple way to handle overdispersion without specifying a distribution for Y_i: we only specify its mean and its variance.

  • The unit is organized in three parts:

    • a motivating example, the Ants data;
    • the theory of quasi-likelihood models;
    • the complete analysis of the example.

  • An alternative, not covered in this course, is to replace the Poisson or the binomial with a different parametric distribution, e.g. the negative binomial.

The content of this Unit is covered in Chapter 6 of Salvan et al. (2020). Alternatively, see Sections 8.1 and 8.3 of Agresti (2015).

A motivating example

The Ants data

  • The Ants dataset of the MLGdata library comes from an experiment run by students of an Australian university (Salvan et al. 2020). The aim is to study which sandwiches meat ants prefer.

  • The explanatory variables are three experimental factors:

    • Bread (4 types): rye, wholemeal, multigrain, white;
    • Filling (3 types): Vegemite, peanut butter, ham and pickles;
    • Butter (2 levels): present (1) or absent (-1).

  • For each of the 4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24 combinations, two pieces of sandwich were placed near the entrance of an ant nest, for a total of n = 48 observations.

  • The response Ant_count is the number of ants on the sandwich after 5 minutes.

Exploratory analysis

  • Filling and Butter seem to matter, whereas Bread has a weaker effect.

  • The response is a count, therefore a natural starting point is a Poisson regression model with logarithmic link, including the Filling-Butter interaction (p = 9).

  • According to the Wald tests, Bread2, Filling3 and both interaction terms are significant at the 5\% level.

  • But can we trust these standard errors?

Is there overdispersion?

  • The residual deviance is D(\bm{y}; \hat{\bm{\mu}}) = 183.05 and the Pearson statistic of Unit B is X^2 = 163.07, both on n - p = 39 degrees of freedom. The p-value of the X^2 test is <0.001.

  • Both statistics are more than four times the degrees of freedom: the data are much more variable than implied by the Poisson model, in which \text{var}(Y_i) = \mu_i.

  • The Poisson model is misspecified and its standard errors are too small: the significant coefficients of the previous slide should not be trusted.

A large X^2 may also be caused by a misspecified mean, e.g. an omitted covariate, a missing interaction or a wrong link function. Correcting the standard errors is appropriate only if the mean structure g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^T\beta is correct.

A word of caution

  • A tempting shortcut is to compare the sample mean and variance of the response. In the Ants data they are 43.6 and 327. Is this enough to conclude that there is overdispersion? No.

  • The Poisson assumption concerns Y_i given the covariates. Consider n = 2000 observations from Y_i \overset{\text{ind}}{\sim} \text{Poisson}(\mu_i), \qquad \log{\mu_i} = 1 + 3 x_i, \qquad x_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(0.5), which have no overdispersion by construction.

  • The simulated data have mean 28.2 and variance 691.2, simply because the covariate shifts the mean. Once the covariate is accounted for, the Poisson fit gives X^2/(n-p) = 1.009.

Back to the Ants data: how can we fix the standard errors, without having to choose a new distribution for the counts?

Quasi-likelihoods

A motivating remark: the linear model

  • In the Gaussian linear model \bm{Y} = \bm{X}\beta + \bm{\epsilon} with \epsilon_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{N}(0, \sigma^2), the maximum likelihood estimator \hat{\beta} = (\bm{X}^T\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^T\bm{y} has the exact distribution \hat{\beta} \sim \text{N}_p(\beta, \sigma^2(\bm{X}^T\bm{X})^{-1}).

  • In Unit A we saw that under the much weaker second-order assumptions \mathbb{E}(\bm{\epsilon}) = \bm{0} and \text{var}(\bm{\epsilon}) = \sigma^2 I_n, the same estimator is still unbiased, with \text{var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2(\bm{X}^T\bm{X})^{-1}, and asymptotically normal.

If the normal model is misspecified but the second-order assumptions hold, the inference based on the normal model remains valid for large n. Gaussianity is only needed for exact results.

  • Question: does the same happen for the maximum likelihood estimator of a GLM?

Likelihood equations of a GLM

  • Let Y_i \overset{\text{ind}}{\sim} \text{ED}(\mu_i, a_i(\phi)v(\mu_i)) with g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^T\beta and a_i(\phi) = \phi/\omega_i. Recall from Unit B that the likelihood equations are \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(y_i - \mu_i)}{\text{var}(Y_i)}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_r} = \frac{1}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i\frac{(y_i - \mu_i)}{v(\mu_i)}\frac{x_{ir}}{g'(\mu_i)} = 0, \qquad r=1,\dots,p.

  • These equations depend on the distribution of Y_i only through \mathbb{E}(Y_i) = \mu_i = g^{-1}(\bm{x}_i^T\beta), \qquad \text{var}(Y_i) = \frac{\phi}{\omega_i}v(\mu_i).

  • Higher-order moments, such as the skewness, have no impact on the equations: any distribution with the same first two moments leads to the same estimate \hat{\beta}. They only affect the distribution of \hat{\beta}, e.g. how quickly it approaches normality.

The quasi-likelihood model

The quasi-likelihood model only assumes the following second-order conditions:

  • (E.1) \quad\mathbb{E}(Y_i) = \mu_i = g^{-1}(\bm{x}_i^T\beta);

  • (E.2) \quad\text{var}(Y_i) = \dfrac{\phi}{\omega_i}v(\mu_i), with \phi > 0 unknown;

  • (E.3) \quad Y_i and Y_j are independent for i \neq j.

  • This is a semiparametric model: only the first two moments of Y_i are specified.

  • The estimate \hat{\beta} is defined as the solution of the estimating equations \sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i\frac{(y_i - \mu_i)}{v(\mu_i)}\frac{x_{ir}}{g'(\mu_i)} = 0, \qquad r=1,\dots,p, which do not depend on \phi and can be solved with the IRLS algorithm of Unit B, whatever the choice of v(\cdot).

Variance inflation

  • For the Gaussian model, (E.2) is the usual homoscedasticity assumption, with \phi = \sigma^2. The novelty concerns counts and proportions, for which the GLM imposes \phi = 1.

  • Keeping the variance function of a GLM, (E.2) inflates its variance by the factor \phi. We have overdispersion if \phi > 1 and underdispersion if \phi < 1, which is far less common.

  • The two most common quasi-likelihood models are:

    • the quasi-Poisson model, with \text{var}(Y_i) = \phi\,\mu_i;
    • the quasi-binomial model, with \text{var}(Y_i) = \phi\,\mu_i(1-\mu_i)/m_i.

  • In both cases the estimating equations are the likelihood equations of the Poisson or binomial GLM, hence \hat{\beta} is the maximum likelihood estimate of the GLM.

As noted in Unit C, the quasi-binomial model makes sense only for grouped data. With binary data (m_i = 1) we necessarily have \text{var}(Y_i) = \mu_i(1-\mu_i), hence \phi = 1.

Where does overdispersion come from?

  • Unobserved heterogeneity. Units with the same covariates may differ because of unrecorded variables. If Y_i \mid U_i \sim \text{Poisson}(U_i\mu_i), with \mathbb{E}(U_i) = 1 and \text{var}(U_i) = \sigma^2, then \mathbb{E}(Y_i) = \mu_i, \qquad \text{var}(Y_i) = \mathbb{E}\{\text{var}(Y_i \mid U_i)\} + \text{var}\{\mathbb{E}(Y_i \mid U_i)\} = \mu_i + \sigma^2\mu_i^2.

  • Correlated trials. Let S_i = m_i Y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m_i} Z_{ij}, with Z_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\mu_i) having correlation \rho > 0, e.g. fetuses of the same litter. Then \text{var}(Y_i) = \frac{\mu_i(1-\mu_i)}{m_i}\{1 + \rho(m_i - 1)\}.

  • In both cases the inflation factor is not constant: variance inflation is only an approximation.

Other variance functions are possible, e.g. v(\mu) = \mu^2 for counts, or v(\mu) = \mu^2(1-\mu)^2 for proportions (Wedderburn 1974), which corresponds to no likelihood. A different v(\cdot) also changes the estimate.

The quasi-score

  • The left-hand side of the estimating equations, divided by \phi, is called quasi-score: q_r(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(Y_i - \mu_i)}{\text{var}(Y_i)}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_r}, \qquad r = 1,\dots,p.

  • Under (E.1)-(E.3) alone, the quasi-score satisfies the Bartlett identities of a genuine score function, that is \mathbb{E}\{q_r(\beta)\} = 0 and \mathbb{E}\{q_r(\beta)q_s(\beta)\} = -\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{\partial q_r(\beta)}{\partial \beta_s}\right\} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\text{var}(Y_i)}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_r}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_s} = [\bm{X}^T\bm{W}\bm{X}]_{rs}, where \bm{W} = \text{diag}(w_1,\dots,w_n) are the weights of Unit B.

The quasi-score behaves like a genuine score, even though no distribution has been specified. This explains the name quasi-likelihood, and it is the key to the asymptotic results.

Properties of \hat{\beta}

Under the assumptions (E.1)-(E.3), the estimator \hat{\beta} is consistent and asymptotically normal: \hat{\beta} \: \dot{\sim} \: \text{N}_p\left(\beta, (\bm{X}^T\bm{W}\bm{X})^{-1}\right), \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{\text{var}(Y_i)g'(\mu_i)^2} = \frac{1}{\phi}\frac{\omega_i}{v(\mu_i) g'(\mu_i)^2}.

  • This is formally identical to the result of Unit B, but it now holds under much weaker assumptions.

  • Does it make sense to talk about the exact distribution of \hat{\beta}? No: it would require the distribution of Y_i, which we deliberately left unspecified.

  • Consistency only requires (E.1): if the mean is correctly specified, \hat{\beta} is consistent even when v(\mu_i) is wrong. The standard errors, however, rely on (E.2).

Estimation of \phi and standard errors

  • The dispersion \phi is estimated with the method of moments estimator of Unit B, which is consistent under (E.1)-(E.3): \hat{\phi} = \frac{X^2}{n - p} = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i \frac{(y_i - \hat{\mu}_i)^2}{v(\hat{\mu}_i)}.

  • The standard errors are obtained from (\bm{X}^T\hat{\bm{W}}\bm{X})^{-1}, plugging in \hat{\beta} and \hat{\phi}. The weights w_i are inversely proportional to \phi.

NoteVariance inflation in a nutshell

When v(\cdot) is the variance function of a GLM, as in the quasi-Poisson and quasi-binomial models:

  1. Fit the ordinary GLM and keep its maximum likelihood estimate \hat{\beta}.

  2. Multiply the standard errors of the GLM by \sqrt{\hat{\phi}}, that is [\widehat{\text{se}}_\text{quasi}(\hat{\beta})]_j = \sqrt{\hat{\phi}}\,[\widehat{\text{se}}_\text{glm}(\hat{\beta})]_j.

The quasi-log-likelihood

  • To compare nested models we need something like a log-likelihood. Under (E.1)-(E.3) we define the quasi-log-likelihood \ell_Q(\beta, \phi) = \sum_{i=1}^n \int_{y_i}^{\mu_i} \frac{\omega_i(y_i - t)}{\phi\,v(t)}\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad \mu_i = g^{-1}(\bm{x}_i^T\beta).

  • Its derivatives are the quasi-score q_r(\beta). Indeed, if F(\cdot) is a primitive of t \mapsto \omega_i(y_i - t)/\{\phi\,v(t)\}, then \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta_r}\{F(\mu_i) - F(y_i)\} = F'(\mu_i)\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_r} = \frac{(y_i - \mu_i)}{\text{var}(Y_i)}\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \beta_r}.

  • In general \ell_Q(\beta, \phi) is not a log-likelihood. However, in the Poisson case (\omega_i = 1, v(t) = t, \phi = 1) we get \ell_Q(\beta, 1) = \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i \log{\mu_i} - \mu_i), up to constants, which is the Poisson log-likelihood.

Comparing nested models

  • The quantity D(\bm{y}; \bm{\mu}) = -2\phi\,\ell_Q(\beta, \phi) is called quasi-deviance. When v(\cdot) is the variance function of a GLM, it coincides with the deviance of Unit B, e.g. the Poisson or the binomial one.

  • Let M_0 \subset M_1 be two nested models, with p_0 and p parameters, and let q = p - p_0. The quasi-log-likelihood ratio statistic is W = 2[\ell_Q(\hat{\beta}, \hat{\phi}) - \ell_Q(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\phi})] = \frac{D(\bm{y}; \hat{\bm{\mu}}_0) - D(\bm{y}; \hat{\bm{\mu}})}{\hat{\phi}} \: \dot{\sim} \: \chi^2_q, where \hat{\phi} is estimated from the larger model.

  • The (quasi-)deviances must not be used as goodness-of-fit statistics, since \phi is unknown.

  • There is no likelihood, therefore the AIC is not available.

Confidence and prediction intervals

  • Consider a sandwich with ham and pickles and butter, whose estimated mean is \hat{\mu}_\text{new} = 66.8 ants. The 95\% intervals are:
Lower Upper
Confidence interval for the mean 57.5 77.5
Prediction interval (quasi-Poisson) 34.5 99.1
Prediction interval (Poisson) 51.0 83.0

  • The confidence interval for \mu is obtained on the linear predictor scale and then transformed.

  • Without a distribution, the prediction interval relies on the approximation Y_\text{new} \: \dot{\sim} \: \text{N}(\hat{\mu}_\text{new}, \hat{\phi}\hat{\mu}_\text{new}).

  • The Poisson prediction interval, based on the Poisson quantiles, is far too narrow.

Summary

  • A quasi-likelihood model only specifies the mean g(\mu_i) = \bm{x}_i^T\beta and the variance \text{var}(Y_i) = (\phi/\omega_i)v(\mu_i) of independent responses.

  • The estimate \hat{\beta} solves the quasi-score equations via IRLS, and it is consistent and asymptotically normal under these weaker assumptions.

  • The dispersion is estimated by \hat{\phi} = X^2/(n-p). In the quasi-Poisson and quasi-binomial models, \hat{\beta} is the GLM estimate and all standard errors are multiplied by \sqrt{\hat{\phi}}.

  • Nested models are compared through the difference of deviances divided by \hat{\phi}. The AIC is not available.

  • In practice, overdispersion is very common with counts and grouped proportions. Ignoring it leads to overconfident conclusions, as in the Ants data.
